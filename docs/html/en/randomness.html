Where the randomness comes from

English translation of docs/randomness.md — the Russian original is the source of truth. Labels inside the diagrams are in Russian.

The noises from which the agents build numbers: how they are taken, how much we credit, how they are mixed, at what speed. On the main screen each noise has its own icon: ⚄ chip RNG, Σ all available, ≋ pulse CSI, ↯ router ping CSI, ⏚ ADC D0, ⧖ RC jitter, ↺ reverse noise, ↕ accelerometer, 🧲 magnetometer. How noise is chosen in an experiment — modes.md.

Board XIAO ESP32-S3, Arduino-ESP32 core 3.3.7 (ESP-IDF 5.5). Raw measurement logs — logs/probe*.log, analysis — tools/probe_report.py, measurement firmware — firmware/entropy_probe.

1. esp_random() and Wi-Fi noise#

According to Espressif's documentation, esp_random() returns true random numbers while Wi-Fi is on: the thermal noise of the radio receiver's ADC is continuously mixed into the hardware generator (2 bits per 80 MHz bus clock). In the compiled library (libesp_hw_support.a, examined with a disassembler):

  • the function reads the generator register 0x6003507C (WDEV_RND_REG);
  • between words it waits 1778 bus clocks (22.2 µs) and XORs together all reads during the wait; hence the limit of ~45,000 words/s (measured: 44,800), the same in any Wi-Fi state;
  • the register itself, without waiting, reads at 10.5 million words/s.

To keep radio noise going into the generator all the time, the firmware keeps Wi-Fi on and without sleep (WiFi.setSleep(false)); the telemetry reports this with the field rf=1.

What cannot be checked. The numbers themselves do not show whether radio noise is in them: the generator has a second source — jitter of the internal RC_FAST oscillator (the DIG_CLK8M_EN bit is set, checked by reading the register). The generator output in four states (Wi-Fi off/on × RC_FAST off/on) was tested for linear complexity (Berlekamp–Massey, 4096 words): in all four it is n/2, as for a random sequence — there is no linear structure, but "with radio noise" cannot be told from "without" by the output. We accept the radio's participation from the documentation and the function code; Wi-Fi noise can be observed directly through CSI.

Rejected: the temperature sensor (two values in 1 °C steps over 4096 reads), Wi-Fi **noise_floor (−97 dBm for all packets), CSI without ping and pulse** (almost no packets addressed to us; in promiscuous mode — 16 packets/s from dozens of different devices).

CSI and 802.11n. When the agent is allowed 802.11n, the router replies with 802.11n packets, and the chip builds CSI only for some of them: 3–82 packets/s for ~95 ping replies (tools/csi_watch.py). With 802.11g CSI comes for every reply — 97–99 per second. That is why prime_lab allows only 802.11b/g (esp_wifi_set_protocol); 54 Mbit/s is more than enough for telemetry. The noise is the same: the low bit is one in 51 % of cases and matches the previous packet in 50 %.

3. How they are mixed#

  • RNG only — the esp_random() words go into the number as they are.
  • Any other set — SHA-256 blocks (the chip's hardware block) of 32 bytes. Into each block every ticked source puts at least 256 credited bits — except the rim sensors: together with others they mix in whatever has accumulated since the previous block (computing does not wait for a slow sensor), and only a sensor alone, without others, waits for its 256 bits. The number stays random even if all sources but one are bad. The sources are gathered in turn and the times add up: for a 256-bit candidate CSI takes ~82 ms, ADC ~49 ms, jitter ~64 ms, RNG 0.17 ms — all four together ~195 ms (~5 candidates/s), 2.4 times slower than CSI alone.

**Noise without hashing (raw).** The hash gathers the scattered randomness of the raw data into a dense 256 bits, but at the same time it stirs away the structure of the source: biases, correlation of neighbouring samples, rhythms. Without the hash one can see whether the physics of the source shows through in the numbers:

  • raw = 1 — the low bits of the raw data go into the number as they are: CSI — the low bit of each data-subcarrier measurement (2–26 and 38–63, 102 bits per packet; the zero, guard and first subcarriers, which the chip sometimes corrupts, are skipped); ADC and jitter — the low bit of a sample; rim sensors — the credited low bits of a byte; RNG — whole words; set B air — whole bytes;
  • raw = 2 — the same with von Neumann debiasing: the pair 01 → 0, 10 → 1, 00 and 11 are dropped. It removes the bias between zeros and ones, but not the correlation of neighbouring samples; four times fewer bits;
  • several ticked sources are combined bitwise (XOR) — the structure of a source is visible when it is ticked alone.

Without the hash the slow sources are faster: on four boards the ADC gives 253 candidates/s (with the hash ~70), CSI about three times more, jitter four times more. The yield of primes without the hash may differ from theory simply because of the bias of the raw data — that is exactly what the mode shows; compare it with the same source with the hash (tools/anomaly_run.py).

  • Not a single composite (the server checked each one), not a single repeat; the yield of primes in all modes is as theory predicts (|z| ≤ 1.1), there is no difference between modes.
  • The only real structure is ADC without hash: the low bit of a sample is 1 more often, and it shows directly in the primes (54 % ones, +34 σ). Von Neumann removes it; CSI and jitter without hash have almost no bias. A one-sided bias hardly affects the sieve and the yield.
  • Residues modulo 3, 5, 7, 11 are uniform; individual p ≈ 0.02–0.04 on hundreds of primes are the expected share for ~40 tests.
  • Gaps between primes in the two-core modes: the spread is smaller than Poisson (coefficient of variation ~0.8 — a prime needs at least one full test) and neighbouring gaps are correlated on the hottest boards (z 5–8) — the trace of the temperature regulator, which works in periods; on cool boards and with slow noise it is absent.
  • In the relay ~9 % of the candidates that survive the sieve go untested when the turn is handed over, so the yield is computed on the tested ones (on the survivors there would be an apparent "shortfall", z −6.8).

5. The primality test#

A candidate of the required length (the top bit and oddness are set) → sieve by small divisors below the threshold td (default 1000) → Miller–Rabin on the first nb prime bases (default 8: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 …). 32 bits — our own code, longer — mbedtls_mpi with the hardware accelerator. For 32 bits every prime found is also checked exactly (bases 2, 7, 61) — this is how "false primes" are counted. The server re-checks the primes it receives (up to 256 bits) with its own Miller–Rabin.

Residues by small divisors are computed with our own code: mbedtls_mpi_mod_int gives a correct residue only for divisors below 2^16 (it divides by half-words); on products of primes up to 2^31 it would lose 84 % of the primes.

One prime falls on roughly ln(2^n)/2 odd candidates: for 256 bits — 88.57, taking into account that the numbers lie in [2²⁵⁵, 2²⁵⁶); the runs give 88.2–88.7. At 256 bits the time per candidate splits as: noise 166 µs (8 words of 22 µs), sieve 43 µs, Miller–Rabin 217 µs. A base test through mbedtls is noticeably slower than the accelerator's pure computation: each call spends time on locking, preparing R² and an interrupt; working with the accelerator directly is a speed reserve.
